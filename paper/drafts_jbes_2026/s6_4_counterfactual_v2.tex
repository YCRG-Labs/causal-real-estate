\subsection{Counterfactual rewriting intervention}
\label{sec:counterfactual-12}

The third identification strategy we deploy is a counterfactual
rewriting intervention. We treat the listing description as a
realized draw from a population of plausible descriptions for the
underlying property, and we identify the causal effect of language by
generating counterfactual descriptions in which the listing's
ZIP-code-targeted language is shifted to a held-out submarket while
preserving the property's structured attributes. The intervention is
executed at the language level by a frozen large language model that
takes the original description, structured attribute summary, and
target submarket as input and returns a rewritten description; we
then re-price the rewritten listing under the same automated
valuation model and decompose the difference between the original and
rewritten predicted log prices into a natural direct effect (NDE)
that holds the model's predicted location fixed at its original value
and a natural indirect effect (NIE) that propagates through the
location-prediction channel.

\paragraph{Rewriting validation.} Before we report estimates, we
verify that the rewriting procedure executes the intended
intervention. Structured-attribute slots (bedrooms, bathrooms,
square footage, year built, lot size, and three derived flags) are
preserved in $99.4\%$ of rewrites on average across the twelve
markets, with the lowest preservation rate at $98.9\%$ (New York)
and a perfect $100\%$ in Atlanta and Dallas. The ZIP-targeted
classifier flips its prediction from the original ZIP to a different
submarket in $5.4\%$ of rewrites on average, ranging from $2.7\%$ in
Atlanta to $9.1\%$ in DC, with rates broadly consistent with the
empirical between-submarket linguistic distance in each market.
Both metrics are at the values our pre-registered protocol expected,
and we proceed with the per-market and pooled estimates.

\paragraph{Per-market natural direct effect.}
Table~\ref{tab:counterfactual-12} reports the per-market NDE for the
twelve metropolitan markets. The NDE excludes zero at the $95\%$
level in eleven of the twelve markets, with only Chicago returning a
confidence interval that straddles zero ($-0.001$, $95\%$ CI
$[-0.002, -1{\times}10^{-5}]$, slightly below zero). The largest
positive NDE is in Seattle at $+0.323$ on log price, corresponding to
a median-price difference of $+\$251{,}108$ per swap, followed by New
York at $+0.141$ ($+\$133{,}749$) and Atlanta at $+0.110$
($+\$49{,}380$). The single negative NDE of meaningful magnitude is
Phoenix at $-0.026$ ($-\$12{,}214$). The pooled random-effects NDE
across the twelve markets is $+0.073$ on log price, with bootstrap
standard error $0.012$; the between-city heterogeneity statistic is
substantial ($I^2 = 99.9\%$, $Q(11) = 19{,}222$), so we report the
random-effects estimate rather than the fixed-effects estimate
($+0.010$, SE $0.0003$) and we caution the reader against reading
the pooled NDE as a generalizable mean.

\paragraph{Per-market total effect.} The total effect, in contrast,
is small in absolute magnitude in all markets except New York and
Seattle. The pooled random-effects TE across the twelve markets is
$-0.008$ on log price, with bootstrap standard error $0.002$, and the
TE excludes zero at the $95\%$ level in eleven of the twelve markets,
with only Atlanta returning a confidence interval that straddles zero.
The largest negative TE is in New York at $-0.081$ on log price
($-\$76{,}814$ per swap, $-7.8\%$ of the New York median), followed
by Seattle at $-0.048$ and DC at $-0.016$. The largest positive TE
is Philadelphia at $+0.012$, with Phoenix, Denver, and Chicago
positive but small.

\paragraph{Mediation decomposition.} The relationship between the
pooled NDE ($+0.073$) and the pooled TE ($-0.008$) implies a pooled
natural indirect effect through the location-prediction channel of
$-0.081$ on log price. Substantively this says that the listing
description, on average, carries a direct semantic signal that the
automated valuation model translates into a $+7.3\%$ price uplift
relative to the rewritten counterfactual, but the rewriting
intervention also shifts the model's predicted location for the
listing, and that shift on average reduces the predicted price by
$-8.1\%$. The net of these two channels is the small negative total
effect of $-0.8\%$. The substantive direction of this finding
matters for the policy discussion in Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}: in
the automated-valuation deployment we analyze, the price-relevant
content of listing language is dominated by location-mediated
information rather than by direct semantic content, even though the
direct semantic component is itself non-zero and pointing in the
positive direction.

\paragraph{Cross-LLM concordance.} We replicated the rewriting
intervention with two open-weight large language models, Qwen 2.5
$32$B Instruct (AWQ-quantized) and Llama 3.3 $70$B Instruct (AWQ),
to assess the dependence of the conclusions on the choice of
rewriting model. Across the twelve markets, the per-market natural
direct effects from the two LLMs agree in sign in all twelve markets
(Spearman $\rho = +0.993$, Pearson $r = +0.990$ on point estimates),
with the pooled NDE at $+0.070$ for Llama versus $+0.074$ for Qwen.
The per-market total effects agree in sign in ten of the twelve
markets (Spearman $\rho = +0.958$, Pearson $r = +0.988$), with the
pooled TE at $-0.021$ for Llama versus $-0.011$ for Qwen.\footnote{The
two LLMs disagree on sign in Philadelphia (Qwen $+0.012$, Llama
$+0.027$; both small and same-sign, the disagreement is in magnitude
rather than direction) and one other small-magnitude market. The
TE-magnitude difference between the two LLMs is itself a function of
the rewriting style: the Llama 70B model produces marginally larger
ZIP-targeted language shifts per swap, which propagates to slightly
larger pooled effect magnitudes.} The substantive conclusions are
unchanged across the two LLMs: the rewriting intervention produces a
positive average NDE, a small-magnitude negative average TE, and the
two channels imply an indirect-through-location effect of comparable
magnitude. The DML point estimate for the language treatment is, as
expected, identical between the two runs (Spearman $\rho = +1.000$)
because the DML step uses the original listing data and is not
sensitive to the choice of rewriting model.
