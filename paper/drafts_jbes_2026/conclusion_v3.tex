
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper has asked whether the predictive gains that text embeddings deliver in
automated valuation models reflect causal semantic content or spatial confounding
mediated through language, and whether the answer travels across metropolitan
housing markets. Working with $69{,}204$ deduplicated Redfin listings across twelve
metropolitan areas, a panel from which we exclude no market and whose
confounders we winsorize at the median plus or minus five median absolute
deviations to neutralize parcel data-entry errors, we built a framework that
joins a partially-linear Double Machine Learning estimator, LEACE concept erasure
with an iterative nonlinear polish, a counterfactual rewriting intervention with
two open-weight language models, and a finite-sample calibration study. What
the framework establishes most centrally is that the price effect of listing text
is not, in the main, geography wearing a semantic costume. Holding the
leading text direction fixed as the treatment and folding a flexible location
basis into the controls leaves its per-standard-deviation effect on log price
almost exactly where it began across eleven of the twelve markets, even though
that same direction provably encodes location. The one place where the
adjustment does bite is New York, whose own text is the most geographic of the
twelve. The effect that survives is robust as well to how the text is encoded,
since a Doc2Vec uniqueness channel pools to $+0.060$ and a sentence-BERT
principal-component axis to $+0.137$, and the two run correlated across the
markets at a Pearson of $+0.94$, so that they track one signal under
two representations rather than two independent observations of it, though that
full-panel agreement is carried by Chicago and Philadelphia and weakens to a
non-significant correlation once the two are set aside. That signal ranges from a small negative
in New York to several times the pooled mean in Chicago and Philadelphia, and a
collinearity-corrected Hotelling test rejects the joint zero null in every one of
the twelve markets. The counterfactual intervention sharpens the reading. A
contrast that strips stylistic content while holding the model's inferred location
fixed isolates a positive style effect of $+0.074$ on log price, while a contrast
that also swaps the targeted submarket nets to approximately zero, the per-market
swap effects dividing in sign across the twelve markets and averaging away rather
than a second channel of comparable size offsetting the first. We
searched for a metropolitan-level demographic moderator of the pooled effect and
found none that survives multiplicity adjustment or leave-one-out leverage
diagnostics at twelve markets, and we report that null rather than a moderator
that an earlier draft had reported before a confounder correction reversed its
sign.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The reading we have offered is hedged in several ways
that are worth stating plainly rather than leaving for a reader to find. The most
consequential is the boundary of the identification itself: the effect is identified
against the location that motivates the paper, which the designs hold fixed, but not
against unmeasured interior quality, which the sensitivity analysis bounds rather than
differences away. The robustness-value analysis of
Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity-12} places that residual on a scale rather than
discharging it. An unobserved confounder able to overturn a per-market estimate
would have to explain more of the residual variation in both the text direction
and log price than the observed spatial controls do, whose partial $R^2$ falls
inside the robustness value in every market but Boston, with the widest margin in
Philadelphia and San Francisco. That assurance is not
uniform. It thins toward the markets
whose robustness values are small in absolute terms, and in Boston, whose estimate
is close to zero to begin with, the spatial control block alone carries more
explanatory power for the treatment than the confounder that would erase the effect,
so nothing in that market's result should be leaned on. We therefore do not claim to have
isolated a structural semantic effect, only to have shown that the location a
representation encodes is largely not the location that moves the price, and to
have bounded how strong any confounding the test cannot see would have to be. What
survives that adjustment need not be semantic content in any broad sense. The
vocabulary that loads on the price end of the text axis is largely condition and
finish language, words for renovation, layout, and quality, and our structured
controls carry bedroom and bathroom counts, area, lot size, and year built but no
direct measure of interior condition or renovation. An unobserved quality signal
that the language proxies would produce the pattern we report, survive the spatial
adjustment, and vary across markets as we find, so we read the surviving effect as
information the text carries beyond location and structure rather than as evidence
that it is priced semantic content as opposed to unmeasured physical quality. A
distinct reading, that the premium is an agent talking a price up rather than
conveying anything about the property, is answered by the sale-price panel of
Appendix~\ref{sec:saleprice}. Were the effect the framing of an asking price it would
attach to the number a seller posts and not to the one a buyer pays, yet it survives
re-estimation on recorded transactions and is individually significant in all ten
markets with public deeds, so what the language moves is validated in the market
rather than set by the seller alone. What the text most plausibly carries, then, is
unit-level condition and finish that the structured attributes do not record and that
buyers pay for, a reading the within-building rung supports by finding the premium
between near-identical units in the same building, where neither neighborhood nor
gross size can vary. Where
an independent assessor measure of condition or improvement value exists, in Boston,
San Francisco, and New York, conditioning on it leaves the effect intact with only
modest attenuation, and in the two markets without one a quality confounder would
have to be several times stronger than any we can measure to overturn the estimate,
so the concern is bounded rather than dispositive; still, our design does more to
separate language from geography than to separate it from quality. The corpus is restricted to the
twelve metropolitan areas served by the Redfin scraper at the time of collection,
which biases the sample toward larger and more competitive markets and away from
the rural and small-metro regimes in which language-mediated spatial confounding
is plausibly different in character. The two embedding channels are collinear, so
their agreement is a robustness check across representation rather than
triangulation across independent biases, and the counterfactual channel, the one
probe with a distinct bias structure, is also the least precise. Finally
the panel is small enough at twelve markets that one high-leverage market, New
York, dominates the moderator diagnostics, so the null moderator result should be
read as the absence of a moderator detectable at this sample size rather than as a
demonstrated absence of moderation.

\paragraph{Implications.} The implication that travels furthest is
methodological, and it cuts against an easy assumption rather than confirming a
dire one. Because a model that prices semantic content and a model that
prices laundered geography make identical predictions, a predictive gain on its
own cannot tell an analyst which of the two it has bought, and the reflexive worry
that the accuracy of a text-augmented valuation is mostly relabeled location is
one that a predictive metric can neither confirm nor dispel. What the results show
is that, once the question is put to a design-based test rather than left to
intuition, the worry largely does not bear out. The text effect survives
adjustment for location in eleven of twelve markets. A practitioner or
regulator who suspected the gains were spatial confounding can therefore be
reassured for the kind of large, competitive metropolitan markets studied here,
while being directed, by the very same test, to the one market where the suspicion
is earned. The lesson is not to distrust text
valuation but to stop certifying it on predictive fit alone. The
deconfounding toggle we describe, holding the text feature fixed while moving
geography across the controls, is a low-cost audit that turns a question of
suspicion into one of measurement before either trust or alarm is allowed to
settle.

\paragraph{Future research.} The natural extensions of this work run along the
seams where its instruments stop short. A panel of substantially
more than twelve markets would give the moderator analysis the leverage it lacks
here and would let the cross-market heterogeneity we document be explained rather
than only described. The counterfactual intervention inherits the rewriting
model's own biases, which a randomized field experiment that asks sellers to
revise descriptions under a pre-registered protocol would replace with a controlled
manipulation at the cost of operational complexity. The realized-transaction panel
of Section~\ref{sec:saleprice} takes a first step against the price-measurement and
asking-price-simultaneity concerns by re-estimating the effect on recorded sale
prices across ten markets, and extending that panel to the markets whose sale
prices are not public would complete it; pairing the causal-residual detector this
paper isolates with the demographic-stratification machinery of the fairness-audit
literature would then let practitioners locate where any language-mediated
mispricing that survives deconfounding is large enough to matter for borrowers.
