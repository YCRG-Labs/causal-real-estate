\subsection{Sentence-BERT principal component channel across twelve markets}
\label{sec:baur-12city}

The Baur, Rosenfelder, and Lutz channel \citep{baur2023analysis}
treats listing language as a $768$-dimensional vector from a frozen
sentence-BERT encoder, extracts the leading principal component as the
treatment variable, and reports its partial effect on log sale price
after partialling out structured property covariates with a
gradient-boosted nuisance learner. The original study reports a
positive per-$\sigma$ effect on a German listing corpus. We replicate
the procedure on twelve U.S. metropolitan markets, retaining their
sentence-BERT model and gradient-boosted nuisances, with influence-
function and pairs-bootstrap inference per
Section~\ref{sec:sim-coverage}.

Table~\ref{tab:baur-12city} reports the per-$\sigma$ PC1 effect for
each market. Four markets identify an effect that excludes zero at
the $95\%$ level, and the signs are mixed across the four. New York
recovers a positive per-$\sigma$ effect of $+0.172$ with confidence
interval $[+0.061, +0.248]$, the largest of the twelve in absolute
magnitude. Philadelphia, Denver, and Atlanta all recover negative
per-$\sigma$ effects of $-0.072$ ($[-0.128, -0.036]$), $-0.034$
($[-0.095, -0.001]$), and $-0.106$ ($[-0.173, -0.003]$) respectively.
The remaining eight markets return point estimates whose confidence
intervals straddle zero.

The mixed-sign pattern across the four significant markets is a
finding in its own right. The leading principal component of a
sentence-BERT representation does not have a uniform meaning across
metropolitan markets in the same way that the Shen-Ross uniqueness
score does. The component direction is chosen by the encoder's
representation of the local listing distribution, which differs in
language register, marketing convention, and structural housing-stock
characteristics across cities. A positive PC1 effect in New York and
a negative PC1 effect in Atlanta are not contradictory under this
reading; they indicate that whatever latent listing characteristic
PC1 captures in each market has opposite implications for price in
the two markets. Section~\ref{sec:baur-pc-sensitivity} explores
this by reporting the leading-component analysis under varying
choices of the principal-component cutoff and finds that the
sign-pattern is stable across the leading three components in
New York and Atlanta but reverses for Philadelphia and Denver at
PC3 and beyond.

\paragraph{Cross-method comparison.} Comparing
Tables~\ref{tab:shen-12city} and \ref{tab:baur-12city} side-by-side,
the two methods agree on a single market. New York is the only city
where the Shen-Ross point estimate ($+0.046$) and the BERT-PC1 point
estimate ($+0.172$) are both positive, although only the BERT-PC1
estimate excludes zero. San Francisco illustrates the opposite
pattern: the Shen-Ross point estimate is large and positive
($+0.131$, excludes zero), while the BERT-PC1 point estimate is
slightly negative ($-0.044$, does not exclude zero), suggesting that
the uniqueness channel and the leading-component channel capture
substantively different aspects of listing language in the San
Francisco market. Section~\ref{sec:cross-method-12} returns to
this orthogonality structure systematically.

\paragraph{Dollarization.} Translating the per-$\sigma$ point
estimates to per-swap dollar amounts at the median sale price gives a
sharper sense of the market-by-market stakes. The New York
PC1 effect of $+0.172$ per $\sigma$ corresponds to a
median-price difference of $\$163{,}136$ per standard-deviation swap,
or $18.8\%$ of the New York median. The Atlanta PC1 effect of
$-0.106$ per $\sigma$ corresponds to $-\$47{,}584$, or $-10.1\%$ of
the Atlanta median. The Philadelphia effect of $-0.072$ corresponds
to $-\$23{,}561$, or $-7.0\%$. These are large fractions of price in
the markets concerned, well above any rounding or normalization
artifact, and they motivate the policy discussion in
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}.
