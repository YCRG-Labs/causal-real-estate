
\subsection{Shen-Ross uniqueness channel across twelve metropolitan markets}
\label{sec:shen-12city}

The Shen-Ross uniqueness channel \citep{shen2021information} measures how far the
language in a listing departs from the language its neighbors use. It quantifies
departure as the cosine distance between each listing's vector-space text
representation and the centroid of its $K = 5$ nearest peers in
latitude-longitude space. We apply the resulting per-listing uniqueness score as
the treatment in a partially-linear DML estimator with cross-fitted ridge
nuisances solved through a Cholesky factorization. This follows the Shen-Ross
uniqueness construction as a close adaptation rather than a verbatim replication
of the original Doc2Vec and MLS-area-by-year cell pipeline. Effects are reported
per standard deviation of the uniqueness score, and the $95\%$ confidence
intervals are formed from the cross-fitting influence-function standard error.

Table~\ref{tab:shen-12city} reports the per-$\sigma$ uniqueness effect for each
of the twelve metropolitan markets, together with the influence-function
confidence interval and the indicator for whether that interval excludes zero.
The panel is well-behaved throughout, with no degenerate market and no exclusion.
The per-market samples range from $986$ deduplicated listings in San Francisco to
$15{,}087$ in New York. The DML interval excludes zero in ten of the twelve
markets, and every one of those ten carries a positive uniqueness premium. The
effect is largest in Chicago at $+0.280$ with interval $[+0.245, +0.315]$
and in Philadelphia at $+0.222$ $[+0.202, +0.242]$. San Francisco follows at
$+0.117$ $[+0.069, +0.165]$. A cluster of mid-sized markets comes next: Dallas
at $+0.082$ $[+0.066, +0.097]$, Denver at $+0.072$ $[+0.055, +0.088]$, Phoenix at
$+0.054$ $[+0.037, +0.070]$, the District of Columbia at $+0.046$
$[+0.025, +0.067]$, and Portland and Seattle both near $+0.042$. Atlanta, the
market on which Shen's identification was originally established, returns a
positive and significant per-$\sigma$ effect of $+0.033$. This is the same sign as her
published Atlanta estimate but a fraction of its magnitude, recovered
under a far more conservative confounder set. The two markets whose intervals
straddle zero are Boston at $+0.020$ $[-0.009, +0.049]$ and New York at $+0.002$
$[-0.010, +0.014]$. New York is the largest market, and its estimate is
indistinguishable from zero. No market returns a significant negative effect. The
panel is uniformly signed on the markets it identifies.

The twelve per-market estimates pool to a positive effect. A Paule-Mandel
random-effects combination with Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman small-sample
intervals places the pooled uniqueness premium at $+0.060$ per standard deviation,
with $95\%$ confidence interval $[+0.018, +0.102]$ and two-sided $p = 0.009$. The
REML variant returns $+0.083$ with $p = 0.006$. The dispersion around this
central tendency is large and is not merely sampling noise. The
heterogeneity statistic is $I^2 = 98.6\%$ with Cochran's $Q = 777$ on eleven
degrees of freedom. Almost all of the variation across markets reflects
true between-market differences in the operative effect rather than estimation
error within markets. Section~\ref{sec:meta-regression-12} takes up this
heterogeneity directly, pooling the twelve estimates by inverse-variance weighting
and scanning for a moderator. We defer that analysis to there rather than
adjudicate it here.

\paragraph{Design analysis.} The cross-market pattern cannot be read as
a symptom of low power, because the panel, free of any degenerate market, is well powered. A Gelman-Carlin
\citep{gelman2014beyond} retrodesign of each per-market estimator uses the
published Shen-Ross Atlanta estimate of $+0.140$ per standard deviation as
the external benchmark true effect. It returns a per-market power at or near $1.00$
in every one of the twelve markets, far above the conventional $0.80$ target.
The per-market influence-function standard errors on samples in the
thousands are small relative to a $0.140$ signal. The Type-S sign-error rate is
negligible throughout, below $10^{-14}$ in every market, so the sign of any
detected effect is reliable. The median Type-M exaggeration factor is $1.00$,
indicating essentially no magnitude inflation in the typical market. San
Francisco, the smallest sample at $986$ listings, carries the mild
anti-conservatism that the analytic variance can show near a thousand
observations. We document this feature directly in the coverage simulation of
Appendix~\ref{app:simulation}. Even there, the retrodesign power against the
$0.140$ benchmark remains high. The design analysis therefore supports reading
both the significant detections and the heterogeneity across markets at face
value.

\paragraph{Magnitudes.} Because the outcome is log listing price, the per-$\sigma$
coefficients translate directly into percentage differences in price. A
one-standard-deviation increase in listing uniqueness corresponds to a price
difference of roughly $+32.3\%$ in Chicago, $+24.8\%$ in Philadelphia, and
$+12.4\%$ in San Francisco. The effect falls through a mid-range of about $+8.5\%$ in
Dallas, $+7.5\%$ in Denver, and $+5.5\%$ in Phoenix. The smallest significant
effects are near $+4\%$ in Washington, Portland, and Seattle and $+3.4\%$ in Atlanta.
The external anchor for these figures is the published Shen-Ross Atlanta
estimate of $+0.140$ per standard deviation, obtained on a sample of roughly
forty thousand listings under MLS-area-by-year fixed effects. Our Atlanta
estimate of $+0.033$ recovers the same sign at a fraction of that
magnitude. The pooled $+0.060$ sits below that single-market benchmark. The
panel as a whole is consistent with a positive Shen-Ross uniqueness premium
that is present, heterogeneous across markets, and smaller on average than the
single-market published benchmark would suggest.
