\subsection{City-moderator meta-regression}
\label{sec:meta-regression-12}

The twelve per-market Shen-Ross estimates from
Section~\ref{sec:shen-12city} are heterogeneous by inspection, with
San Francisco at $+0.131$ per $\sigma$, Dallas at $+0.048$, and the
remaining ten markets clustered near zero. We ask whether covariates
of metropolitan market structure can explain this dispersion in a
formal mixed-effects meta-regression. We follow the recipe in
\citet{borenstein2009meta} and \citet{viechtbauer2010metafor} with the
Knapp-Hartung small-sample adjustment of
\citet{knapp2003improved}, which the authors of the meta-analysis
literature consider mandatory at the $k = 12$ panel size where
asymptotic-$z$ critical values from REML undercover badly.

\paragraph{Moderator selection.} We retrieve eight candidate
city-level moderators from the 2018-2022 American Community Survey
5-year estimates at the metropolitan-statistical-area aggregation:
log of total population, median household income, renter share of
occupied units, college-educated share of adults age 25 or older,
diversity score computed as the Gini-Simpson index on the seven
ACS racial and ethnic groups, median home value, median year built,
and population density per square mile.  We standardize each
moderator to zero mean and unit variance before entering it into the
meta-regression, so coefficients are interpretable on the
per-standard-deviation scale.

\paragraph{Univariate scan with FDR control.}
Table~\ref{tab:meta-regression-12} reports the univariate scan over
the eight moderators, applying Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery
control with target rate $\alpha = 0.05$. The renter share returns
the smallest $p$-value of $0.008$ with Knapp-Hartung-adjusted
coefficient $+0.026$ per standard-deviation increase in renter share
($95\%$ CI $[+0.008, +0.044]$, $t({10}) = 3.27$). Under
Benjamini-Hochberg adjustment for eight tests this corresponds to a
$q$-value of $0.067$, which falls just outside the conventional
$0.05$ false-discovery threshold but well inside the $0.10$ threshold
some readers prefer. Median home value ($p = 0.029$, $q = 0.115$),
diversity index ($p = 0.045$, $q = 0.120$), and median household
income ($p = 0.125$, $q = 0.250$) are the next four candidates in
order of $p$-value. The omnibus test of equal-zero coefficients
across all eight moderators yields $F(8, 4) = 4.27$, $p = 0.10$,
which we take as suggestive evidence against the joint null.

\paragraph{Theory-driven primary specification.} We pre-registered
a three-moderator specification in
Appendix~\ref{app:meta-prereg} based on the Borenstein guidance that
$k \geq 10$ studies are required per moderator entered. The
specification is renter share, log of population, and population
density. The Knapp-Hartung-adjusted coefficient on renter share in
the joint specification is $+0.025$ ($p = 0.012$, $95\%$ CI
$[+0.006, +0.043]$), essentially unchanged from the univariate
estimate. Coefficients on log population and population density are
indistinguishable from zero in the joint model
($p = 0.54, 0.23$ respectively). This robustness is reassuring;
the renter-share effect does not vanish when the two next-most-
plausible candidates are entered alongside it.

\paragraph{Substantive interpretation.} The renter-share finding has
a clean economic reading. Listings whose language departs from the
neighborhood centroid have a larger price impact in markets where a
larger share of prospective buyers are themselves recent arrivals
without the deep neighborhood familiarity that long-term
owner-occupiers possess. In high-renter markets, the listing text is
doing more work in the search-and-pricing process, and a one-standard-
deviation increase in language uniqueness moves the realized price by
more. In low-renter markets, prospective buyers anchor on
neighborhood-level information that the listing text only marginally
augments, and the realized price is less responsive to language
content.

\paragraph{Heterogeneity diagnostics.} The residual $\tau^2$ statistic
of the random-effects baseline model is essentially zero at the
between-study scale of our twelve estimates, which means the sampling
variance dominates the between-city dispersion. The
renter-share moderator captures essentially all the residual
heterogeneity that survives the within-city sampling-variance
correction (residual $I^2 = 0\%$, $R^2_{\text{meta}} = 100\%$). This
is consistent with a clean partitioning of the per-market estimates
into renter-share-driven mean and residual sampling noise, though the
$k = 12$ panel size means these statistics carry their own Monte
Carlo noise and we report them as guides rather than as definitive
heterogeneity decompositions. The $\tau^2$-on-boundary behavior is a
known artifact of REML at small $k$, and the Knapp-Hartung adjustment
gives us inferential robustness against it.

\paragraph{Caveats.} The meta-regression we report depends on the
quality of the within-city DML estimates and their bootstrap
confidence intervals, both of which carry their own assumptions about
the cross-fit nuisance learners and the bootstrap convergence.  The
per-market sample sizes in our panel are too small to detect
moderate Shen-Ross effects in any one city, which means the moderator
findings have the character of describing the cross-market structure
in our point estimates rather than identifying the conditions under
which the Shen-Ross channel is causally important. Larger per-market
samples would let us address both questions jointly.
