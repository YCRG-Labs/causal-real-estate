\subsection{Sentence-BERT principal component channel across twelve markets}
\label{sec:baur-12city}

The Baur, Rosenfelder, and Lutz channel \citep{baur2023analysis} treats
listing language as a $768$-dimensional vector from a frozen sentence-BERT
encoder and uses the leading principal component direction as a scalar
treatment in a partially-linear DML estimator. The original study computes the
PCA per market on the local listing distribution. On a single-market analysis
this is the canonical choice, but on a twelve-market panel it produces a
treatment whose direction is identified only locally and only up to a sign, so
per-market PC1 estimates can carry opposite signs across markets even when the
underlying language-price relationship is direction-consistent. A panel built
on per-market axes therefore mixes genuine heterogeneity with an artifact of
local sign and orientation, and it cannot be pooled coherently.

\paragraph{Pooled within-city-centered PCA.} To obtain a treatment definition
that is comparable across markets, we replace the per-market PCAs with a single
global principal-component direction extracted from the pooled within-city
covariance of the stacked embeddings. We center each city's embeddings at its
own mean before pooling, which removes between-city vocabulary differences from
the global covariance and produces a leading eigenvector identified as the
within-city common axis. This is the standard pooling for cross-population PCA
discussed in \citet{flury1984}, equivalent in construction to a linear
discriminant analysis pooled covariance. We do not standardize individual
embedding dimensions before pooling because sentence-BERT outputs are
L2-normalized by design and per-dimension scaling would inflate the anisotropic
noise structure documented by \citet{ethayarajh2019contextual}. We pin the sign
of the leading eigenvector by the positive-sum-of-loadings convention and
$z$-score the per-listing projection within each city, so the estimand on each
market remains a per-city-standard-deviation move along the common axis. The
global axis is extracted from the within-city-centered pooling of all twelve
markets, an analysis sample of $69{,}173$ listings whose per-market sizes range
from $986$ in San Francisco to $15{,}227$ in New York.

The leading axis captures a modest share of the total variance in the pooled
embedding matrix, consistent with the anisotropic clustering of sentence-BERT
representations documented by \citet{ethayarajh2019contextual}. Per-listing
scores along it separate utilitarian and as-is language at one end from
amenity-rich and luxury language at the other, so the sign of the
per-standard-deviation DML estimate should be negative if listings whose
language sits toward the utilitarian end tend to transact at lower prices, which
is the pattern the hedonic setup leads us to expect.

\paragraph{Per-market estimates.} Table~\ref{tab:baur-12city} reports the
per-market per-standard-deviation effect under the pooled-PCA treatment. The
corrected definition removes the sign ambiguity of the per-market axes and
delivers a single direction of effect across the panel. Eleven of the twelve
markets return a negative point estimate, and the twelfth, New York, returns a
near-zero positive estimate of $+0.011$ with a confidence interval
$[-0.001, +0.024]$ that includes zero. Eight of the twelve markets return a
confidence interval that excludes zero at the $95\%$ level, and every one of
those eight is negative. Philadelphia carries the largest effect at
$\hat\theta = -0.310$ with interval $[-0.331, -0.288]$, followed by Dallas at
$-0.119$, Chicago at $-0.117$, and Atlanta at $-0.115$. San Francisco returns
$-0.101$ with interval $[-0.151, -0.051]$, and Boston, Portland, and Denver
return smaller but still significant effects of $-0.049$, $-0.043$, and
$-0.035$. The four markets whose intervals span zero, namely New York, the
District of Columbia at $-0.019$, Phoenix at $-0.021$, and Seattle at $-0.024$,
all carry small point estimates. Portland, which the per-market PCA had rendered
a degenerate outlier, now sits squarely inside the panel at $-0.043$ with
interval $[-0.066, -0.020]$, so the panel is clean at twelve markets and there
is no market we exclude.

\paragraph{Pooled inference.} Section~\ref{sec:meta-regression-12} takes up the
pooled inference question directly via random-effects meta-analysis of the
twelve per-market estimates. Combining the estimates with a Paule-Mandel
$\tau^2$ and the Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman variance correction returns a
pooled per-standard-deviation effect of $-0.078$ with standard error $0.025$,
a $t$ statistic of $-3.16$ on $11$ degrees of freedom, a two-sided $p$ of
$0.009$, and a $95\%$ confidence interval of $[-0.133, -0.024]$. The pooled
estimate is small but statistically detectable and negative across the panel.
Heterogeneity is large, with $Q = 755$ on $11$ degrees of freedom, an $I^2$ of
roughly $98.5\%$, and a Paule-Mandel $\tau^2$ of about $0.0072$, so the pooled
point estimate masks substantial cross-market dispersion that Philadelphia
anchors at one end and New York at the other. Because between-market variance
dwarfs the within-market sampling variance, the meta-analytic weights are close
to uniform and the pooled estimate coincides almost exactly with the unweighted
across-market mean. We attempted a single pooled DML estimator with city fixed
effects and the Chiang-Kato-Ma-Sasaki \citep{chiang2022multiway} cluster-robust
variance, but the small number of clusters produced fold-assignment instability
in the cross-fitted ridge nuisances that we judged not robust enough for a
publication headline, with pooled point estimates that shifted materially across
independent invocations on the same seed and data. The random-effects
meta-analysis treats each market's $\hat\theta_i$ as a sufficient statistic and
combines them via inverse-variance weighting with a Paule-Mandel $\tau^2$
estimate, which is the canonical small-$G$ alternative to cluster-respecting
cross-fitting \citep{veroniki2016methods}.

\paragraph{Magnitude and interpretation.} The pooled per-standard-deviation
effect of $-0.078$ on log sale price is about a seven and a half percent
reduction in price, which is small as a fraction of value but substantial
against the magnitudes published in the housing and text-as-treatment
literatures. A single additional bedroom in the canonical
\citet{sirmans2005composition} meta-analysis of forty-five hedonic studies
carries a mean effect of $+3.8\%$ on log price, so the pooled text-axis effect
is roughly twice the marginal price of an additional bedroom in magnitude, and
it exceeds the racial sale-price premium of approximately three percent that
\citet{bayer2017racial} document for Black and Hispanic homebuyers across
Chicago, Baltimore-Washington, San Francisco, and Los Angeles. The more
informative comparison is internal. The Doc2Vec uniqueness construct of
\citet{shen2021information} runs in the same direction the original author
reports and the opposite direction from our pooled BERT-PC1 effect. In San
Francisco, where both channels are estimated on the identical $986$-listing
sample, the same DML pipeline recovers a uniqueness effect of $+0.111$ with
interval $[+0.063, +0.159]$, positive and significant, while the BERT-PC1 axis
returns $-0.101$ with interval $[-0.151, -0.051]$, negative and significant.
Pooled across the twelve markets the uniqueness effect remains positive at
about $+0.04$ per standard deviation, against the $-0.078$ of the BERT axis.
The two text treatments therefore disagree in sign both within a single market
and across the panel, which is the central reading of this section. Textual
distinctiveness in the Shen sense and the utilitarian-to-amenity axis that the
leading sentence-BERT component recovers are different constructs that price
differently, and the sign disagreement is a substantive finding about what each
representation measures rather than an artifact of estimation.
