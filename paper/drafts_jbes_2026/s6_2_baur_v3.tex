
\subsection{Sentence-BERT principal component channel across twelve metropolitan markets}
\label{sec:baur-12city}

The Baur, Rosenfelder, and Lutz channel \citep{baur2023automated} treats
listing language as a $768$-dimensional vector from a frozen sentence-BERT
encoder and uses the leading principal component direction as a scalar
treatment in a partially-linear DML estimator. The original study computes the
PCA per market on the local listing distribution. On a single-market analysis
this is the canonical choice, but on a twelve-market panel it produces a
treatment whose direction is identified only locally and only up to a sign, so
per-market PC1 estimates can carry opposite signs across markets even when the
underlying language-price relationship is direction-consistent. A panel built
on per-market axes therefore mixes true heterogeneity with an accident of
local sign and orientation, and it cannot be pooled coherently.

\paragraph{Pooled within-city-centered PCA.} To obtain a treatment definition
that is comparable across markets, we replace the per-market PCAs with a single
global principal-component direction extracted from the pooled within-city
covariance of the stacked embeddings. We center each city's embeddings at its
own mean before pooling, which removes between-city vocabulary differences from
the global covariance and produces a leading eigenvector identified as the
within-city common axis. This is the standard pooling for cross-population PCA
discussed in \citet{flury1984}, equivalent in construction to a linear
discriminant analysis pooled covariance. We do not standardize individual
embedding dimensions before pooling because sentence-BERT outputs are
L2-normalized by design and per-dimension scaling would inflate the anisotropic
noise structure documented by \citet{ethayarajh2019contextual}. We $z$-score the
per-listing projection within each city, so the estimand on each market is a
per-city-standard-deviation move along the common axis. The global axis is
extracted from the within-city-centered pooling of all twelve markets, an
analysis sample of $69{,}204$ listings whose per-market sizes range from $986$ in
San Francisco to $15{,}255$ in New York.

\paragraph{Sign convention.} A principal component and its negation span the
same axis, so the sign of the per-standard-deviation effect is a convention
rather than a finding. We adopt the convention that orients the common axis so
that the pooled effect on log listing price is non-negative, which renders the
per-market estimates sign-comparable to the hedonic uniqueness channel of
Section~\ref{sec:shen-12city} and to the counterfactual differential of
Appendix~\ref{sec:counterfactual-12}. Under the opposite convention every sign
reported below reverses; the magnitudes, the cross-market dispersion, and the
concordance with the other channels are invariant to it. What the orientation
does not do is manufacture agreement between channels, a point we develop in
Section~\ref{sec:cross-method-12}, because the across-market correlation between
the BERT axis and the hedonic channel is invariant in magnitude under any global
sign flip of either one.

\paragraph{Per-market estimates.} Table~\ref{tab:baur-12city} reports the
per-market per-standard-deviation effect under the oriented pooled-PCA
treatment. The corrected definition removes the sign ambiguity of the per-market
axes and delivers a single direction of effect across the panel. Eleven of the
twelve markets return a confidence interval that excludes zero at the $95\%$
level, and eleven carry a positive point estimate. New York is the
sole negative, at $-0.016$ on $[-0.031, -0.002]$, a small effect that is
statistically distinguishable from zero but economically close to it, and Seattle
is the one market whose interval covers zero, at $+0.030$ on
$[-0.002, +0.062]$. Chicago and Philadelphia anchor the high end at $+0.464$ on
$[+0.428, +0.501]$ and $+0.374$ on $[+0.356, +0.392]$, followed by the District
of Columbia at $+0.151$ and Dallas at $+0.142$. Atlanta sits at $+0.134$ and
San Francisco at $+0.106$ on $[+0.056, +0.155]$, and Portland, Boston, and Denver
fall between $+0.061$ and $+0.076$, while Phoenix at $+0.055$ and Seattle at
$+0.030$ return the smallest positive effects. Portland, which the per-market PCA had
rendered a degenerate outlier, now sits squarely inside the panel at $+0.076$ on
$[+0.051, +0.101]$, so the panel is well-behaved at twelve markets and there is no
market we exclude. The per-market intervals are tight
because the pooled-PCA treatment is estimated on the full within-market sample,
which ranges into the thousands of listings, rather than on the description
subset.

\paragraph{Pooled inference.} Pooling the twelve per-market estimates by
inverse-variance weighting returns a per-standard-deviation effect of $+0.137$ with
standard error $0.041$ and a $95\%$ interval of $[+0.048, +0.227]$, detectable and
positive across the panel under the adopted orientation. Between-market dispersion
is large, anchored by Chicago and Philadelphia at one end and New York at the other,
so the pooled point masks a wide distribution and the precision weights sit close to
uniform. The field-standard alternative, a single stacked DML estimator with city
fixed effects and the \citet{chiang2022multiway} cluster-robust variance, proved
unstable at twelve clusters, with fold-assignment sensitivity in the cross-fitted
nuisances and pooled points that shifted across invocations on the same seed and
data, so we combine the per-market estimates directly and report the stacked fit
only as a check. Section~\ref{sec:meta-regression-12} develops the pooled figures
and the null moderator scan. Because listing text clusters geographically, the
influence-function interval, which treats within-market listings as independent, is
too narrow; clustering the influence function at the zip level, or applying a Conley
spatial kernel on the coordinates, widens the per-market intervals by roughly forty
percent, and the effect nonetheless remains significant in every market where the
independent interval already excluded zero, so accounting for spatial dependence does
not disturb the pattern (Appendix~\ref{app:simulation}).

\paragraph{Magnitude and interpretation.} The pooled per-standard-deviation
effect of $+0.137$ on log listing price is about a fifteen percent change in price,
which is small as a fraction of value but substantial against the magnitudes
published in the housing and text-as-treatment literatures. A single additional
bedroom in the canonical \citet{sirmans2005composition} meta-analysis of
forty-five hedonic studies carries a mean effect of $+3.8\%$ on log price, so that
if the association were read as an effect it would be several times the marginal
price of an additional bedroom, a scale comparison we intend as illustrative
rather than as a claim that a standard-deviation move along a text axis is a
manipulable quantity.
The more informative comparison is internal. The Doc2Vec uniqueness construct of
\citet{shen2021information} runs in the same direction as the oriented BERT axis,
both within markets and across the panel. In San Francisco, where both channels
are estimated on the identical sample, the same DML pipeline recovers a
uniqueness effect of $+0.124$ and a BERT-PC1 effect of $+0.106$, and pooled across
the twelve markets the uniqueness effect is about $+0.060$ against the $+0.137$ of
the BERT axis. The two text treatments therefore concord in sign and in
cross-market ranking, with the BERT axis roughly twice the hedonic channel in
pooled magnitude. We are explicit about what this concordance does and does not
establish. The two channels are alternative encodings of the same listing text
applied to the same listings under the same confounder adjustment, and
Section~\ref{sec:cross-method-12} shows they are strongly correlated across
markets, so their agreement is evidence that the text-on-price signal is robust to
the choice of text representation rather than evidence from two independent
sources. Textual distinctiveness in the Shen sense and the utilitarian-to-amenity
axis that the leading sentence-BERT component recovers price in the same
direction, and the difference between them is one of magnitude and of which
markets each detects, not of sign.

\paragraph{What the leading axis prices.} To make concrete what the leading axis
encodes, we regress each listing's projection onto it on a TF-IDF vocabulary of
the description, pooled across the twelve markets, and read off the terms with the
largest coefficients. Table~\ref{tab:loadings} reports them. The high-price end of
the axis is dominated by property-quality and amenity language, words like
\emph{spacious}, \emph{modern}, \emph{designed}, \emph{chef}, \emph{owner suite},
and \emph{great room} that describe condition, finish, and layout rather than
location. The low-price end is dominated by land and transactional boilerplate,
\emph{vacant}, \emph{lot}, \emph{acres}, \emph{zoned}, \emph{hoa}, \emph{mls}, and
\emph{seller}, the vocabulary of undeveloped parcels and the mechanics of a sale
rather than of a home. The axis does also carry some lexical geography:
premium-neighborhood terms such as \emph{center city} and \emph{rittenhouse
square} load near the top, and the bare city names that head the pooled list
reflect between-market price differences that the within-city centering only
partly removes. This is the mixture the deconfounding test of
Section~\ref{sec:decomposition} is built to separate. The axis encodes both the
semantic content of a property and the neighborhood it sits in, and holding
location fixed is what shows that it is the quality language, not the geography,
that carries the price effect.
